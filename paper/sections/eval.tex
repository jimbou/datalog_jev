\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

Our experiments address five research questions:
\begin{description}
\item[RQ1 (Effectiveness):] How many hole-dependent clean verdicts do priced receipts clear at a certified risk, at what cost in missed leaks, compared with the baselines?
\item[RQ2 (Validity):] Does the certified error hold when the answer key, the pricer and the calibration parameters change?
\item[RQ3 (Ablations):] Which components of the approach decide how much is cleared?
\item[RQ4 (Checking):] Can checking a few receipt facts clear more verdicts?
\item[RQ5 (Beyond the calibration setting):] Does a threshold certified on F-Droid apps hold on other populations and other kinds of holes?
\end{description}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Apps.}
We selected 585 open-source apps from F-Droid and built flow graphs for the 332 that fit the full analysis within the budget of Section~\ref{sec:impl}; they are the \emph{main corpus}.
In 307 of them at least one clean sink depends on holes.
These apps contain 28{,}206 clean, hole-dependent sinks whose status the main key decides: 22{,}888 are real leaks that the analysis misses, and 5{,}318, in 298 apps, never receive sensitive data.
We call these 5{,}318 sinks the \emph{hard slice}: they are leak-free, but a sound treatment of unmodeled methods cannot clear them.
The fallback for large apps builds 110 more apps, 66 of them with decided hole-dependent sinks; they are used in RQ5.
For RQ5 we also use TaintBench~\cite{taintbench}, 39 malware apps with 203 documented leaks, for 33 of which we built graphs.

\paragraph{Answer keys.}
A wrong clearance is defined relative to a key, so we use several keys built in different ways.
\begin{itemize}
\item The \emph{main key} uses FlowDroid's hand-written models where they exist. For the methods FlowDroid does not model, it uses the labels on which two language models from different families, Sonnet~5 and GPT-6.1, agree when both are given the written definition of Section~\ref{sec:flow}; where they disagree, it uses K2 (below) if K2 decides the fact, and otherwise leaves the fact unknown.
\item The \emph{deletion keys} contain no language-model labels. They take FlowDroid's models as the truth and delete a share of the modeled classes; the deleted classes become the holes, priced as if unmodeled, and all other modeled classes stay code. Deleting whole classes prevents a pricer from copying the answer of a sibling method.
\item \emph{K2} uses summaries that StubDroid~\cite{stubdroid} generates from the Android~13 and Java~8 source code.
\item The \emph{Sonnet} and \emph{GPT} keys label the unmodeled methods with one model each, asked a short question without the written definition.
\item The \emph{audited key} replaces the labels of the 446 facts that all accepted verdicts rest on by the answers of an audit, in which a person read the Android source code of 67 facts. \todo{G2: a second rater checks 30--40 of these facts; report Cohen's $\kappa$ between raters and against the key.}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Pricers and baselines.}
Every results table reports the same set of methods in the same order.
Two anchors bound the possible outcomes: the \emph{sound} treatment, which clears nothing, and \emph{models only}, which trusts every clean verdict.
Four baselines stand for the prior answers of Section~\ref{sec:intro}: \emph{counting} assumptions, a language model's answer \emph{taken as fact}, the \emph{uncalibrated bound} with $\delta=\alpha$, and \emph{conformal selection} over verdicts.
Our approach is reported with four pricers: a frequency prior, a feature classifier, Sonnet~5 and Jev (Section~\ref{sec:impl}); Jev is the main pricer and Sonnet~5 the rerunnable one.
Five cheaper language models appear only in the pricer ablation (RQ3).

\paragraph{Protocol and default configuration.}
Unless a table says otherwise, every number uses the 307 apps of the main corpus, the main key, Jev, $\alpha=0.05$ and $\varepsilon=0.1$.
We split the apps at random into two halves 200 times; in each split we certify a threshold on one half (candidate thresholds from 0.01 to 0.5) and measure on the other half the share of the hard slice that is cleared and the share of apps with at least one wrong clearance.
We report means over the 200 splits, with the 5th to 95th percentile where the spread matters.
The guarantee concerns the second number: it should stay below $\alpha$.
Each experiment varies one factor of this configuration.

Table~\ref{tab:main} compares all methods under the default configuration, and Figure~\ref{fig:curve} shows the whole trade-off between clearing and wrong clearances.
With Jev's prices, receipts clear 48.7\% of the hard slice at $\alpha=0.05$ while 0.9\% of held-out apps receive a wrong clearance, and 60.0\% at $\alpha=0.10$; at $\alpha=0.02$ they still clear 37.0\% (0.3\% of apps wrong).
In every receipt row the observed error stays below $\alpha$, as Proposition~\ref{prop:ltt} predicts.
Trusting FlowDroid's models alone, the usual practice, clears the whole hard slice but misses a real leak through an unmodeled method in 93\% of the apps.

\emph{What the user sees.}
At the threshold certified on all apps (0.2), opening unmodeled methods adds 28{,}206 decided alarms, of which 81\% are real leaks; clearing raises this share to 89\% and wrongly clears one real leak, so the analysis reports 22{,}887 of the 22{,}888 real leaks that FlowDroid's models miss (real on our flow graph, which is less precise than FlowDroid's; Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
Per app, the false alarms drop from a median of 13 to 6 (90th percentile: 36 to 20), but only 5 of 298 apps lose all of them, and a remaining false alarm rests on a median of three facts.
Receipts therefore halve the triage work rather than remove it; checking facts (RQ4) clears more.

\emph{Each baseline fails in its own way.}
Counting gives every receipt of size one the same bound, so the first threshold already clears every one-fact receipt, which puts a wrong clearance in 87\% of the apps, and the calibration refuses every threshold: prices are what make receipts usable.
Taking a language model's yes/no answers as library models has one operating point whose error cannot be chosen: on the deletion keys it clears 80 to 90\% of the hard slice, but 3.5\% of the apps receive a wrong clearance on average and up to 7.7\% in some runs, above a 5\% target in 9 of 18 runs.
On the main key the same baseline is worse with Jev's answers at 0.5: it clears 96.4\% of the hard slice but leaves a wrong clearance in 57\% of the apps, because Jev answers ``no'' to many methods that do propagate. GPT-6.1's rubric labels, read as models (``undecidable'' as no model, as FlowDroid treats a missing model), clear 99.6\% with 3.3\% of the apps wrong; this is flattering, since these labels are half of the main key, and still the error exceeds 5\% in 11 of the 200 held-out halves (95th percentile 5.2\%), with no knob to lower it.
Using the bound directly as a probability is safe for Jev but clears only 11.8\% (25.2\% at $\alpha=0.10$), because Jev's prices are conservative.
Conformal selection clears the most, 74.8\%, but misses its own target, with 6.6\% of the cleared verdicts wrong at a 5\% target (18.0\% at 10\%), because verdicts in one app share facts and the apps are not alike; its wrong verdicts are spread over 53 of the 307 apps, so a user reading one app's report would see a wrong clearance in one app out of six.
RQ3 returns to the unit of the guarantee.

\emph{Cost.}
Building the flow graph is the expensive step: within FlowDroid's budget, a build takes a median of 25\,s per app (90th percentile 489\,s; FlowDroid 10\,s, on fewer apps, Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
The pricer is asked once per method, not per app: the 7{,}530 unmodeled methods of the corpus cost \$0.21 with Jev and \$7.27 with Sonnet~5, an app alone needs a median of 1{,}067 of them (\$0.03 and \$1.05), and a new app adds a median of one.
Receipts for all 332 apps take under five minutes on four cores, 200 calibrations 5\,s, and the answer key one call per method and labeling model, once.

\answer{\textbf{RQ1:} At a 5\% target, priced receipts clear 49\% of the false alarms that opening unmodeled methods adds, keep all but one of the real leaks that FlowDroid's models miss, and give 0.9\% of held-out apps a wrong clearance. Counting assumptions clears nothing, a model taken as fact has no risk control, and per-verdict selection misses its own target.}

The guarantee holds under all eight keys: the share of held-out apps with a wrong clearance stays at or below 2.3\% at $\alpha=0.05$.
The cleared share depends on the key, from about 25\% under the keys with single-model labels to 49\% under the main key and 93\% under K2, which decides fewer facts and calls most of them ``no''.
Flipping 11\% of the Sonnet key's ``no'' labels at random, the error rate a person's check of these labels suggested, lowers the cleared share to a few percent without breaking the guarantee.
The result also changes little with the confidence parameter, the size of the calibration half, or when apps that bundle the same libraries are kept on the same side of each split (Table~\ref{tab:unit}).
In a few splits the held-out share of wrong apps exceeds 5\%; the guarantee bounds how often this happens, at most a share $\varepsilon$ of calibrations, not each split.
The same holds for every pricer of RQ3, including those that clear nothing: a poor pricer costs clearance, not validity.

\emph{Is the key independent of the pricer?}
Sonnet~5 is both a pricer and one of the two labelers of the main key.
Under the key built from Sonnet's own labels alone, Sonnet clears less than under the main key (9.1\% against 16.7\%), and under the keys with no Sonnet labels, the deletion keys and K2, it clears as much as Jev or more, so agreement with itself does not explain its results.

\emph{Does the key follow the definition?}
The disagreement between Sonnet and GPT comes from the question, not from the models.
Asked the short question, the two models agree on 81\% of the candidate flows (Cohen's $\kappa=0.62$) and with a person's source-code checks on 81\% and 90\% of the facts.
Asked again with the written definition and one reason code per answer (Appendix~\ref{app:flow}), they agree on 90\% of 37{,}160 candidate flows ($\kappa=0.80$), both call about 41\% of the flows real, and they agree with the person on 92\% and 93\% of the 60 facts; on the 57 facts where both models give the same answer, the person agrees with that answer 54 times (95\%).
This is why the main key uses the answers on which both models agree under the written definition.
\todo{G2: agreement between the two human raters.}

\answer{\textbf{RQ2:} The certified error stays below the target under all eight keys, including keys with no language-model labels and keys with random noise, under every pricer, and under every calibration setting we tried. Only the cleared share moves.}

\emph{The unit of the guarantee.}
Controlling the error per verdict clears more but, as RQ1 showed, leaves a wrong clearance in one app out of six, because verdicts within an app share facts.
The per-program unit is what makes the guarantee meaningful to a user who reads one report.

\emph{The pricer.}
Table~\ref{tab:panel} adds five cheaper language models, asked the same question as Sonnet.
None of them clears much, and the reason is visible in their prices: they answer almost only with extreme values (Qwen3.8-27B gives 0.02 or 0.95 to 97\% of the flows) and give the lowest price to some real flows.
The lowest threshold that clears anything then already puts a wrong clearance in many apps, and the calibration refuses it.
Average ranking quality does not predict this.
Table~\ref{tab:rank} shows that the classifier and Sonnet rank verdicts almost as well as Jev on average (AUROC 0.965 and 0.950 against 0.970), yet clear far less (24\% and 17\% against 49\%).
By Proposition~\ref{prop:order}, only the order of the bounds matters, and only at the low end, where verdicts are cleared: one real flow with a low price is enough to make an app wrong.
The deletion keys (Figure~\ref{fig:deletion} in the appendix) show a second property: a classifier trained on FlowDroid's models is strong when single methods are deleted, because it learns the conventions of the remaining sibling methods, and collapses when whole classes are deleted, which is the realistic case, since 86\% of the unmodeled methods that appear in receipts belong to classes FlowDroid does not model at all.
Language-model pricers do not depend on siblings and keep their clearance.

\emph{The question.}
Jev's clearance is 8.5\% when asked whether taint propagates and 48.7\% when the question states that a tainted object taints everything read from it; asked only whether a flow exists, it is certified in 2 of 200 splits.
Without the rule, getters on tainted objects receive low prices, and the few real flows among them block the threshold.

\emph{The combination of prices.}
With the same receipts, summing over distinct facts instead of edges clears 51.7\%, the largest single price 33.7\%, and the product under independence 52.3\%, all with at most 1.8\% of apps wrong.
The calibration makes each of these valid; the union bound is the one that is also a guaranteed upper bound for each verdict.

\emph{Pricer variants.}
To improve the pricer without choosing on the test data, we fixed a development subset of 92 apps and try each variant there first; every variant tried is reported.
Jev with the full written definition in the question clears the same as the main wording (39.0\% against 38.4\% on the development apps).
Sonnet~5.5 asked with the written definition (three samples, the highest price kept) clears 43.8\%, and five combinations of existing prices that need no new calls clear between 36.5\% and 47.9\%; the best, the higher of Jev's and Sonnet~5.5's prices, was chosen.
On the other 215 apps of the corpus it clears 42.8\% (1.4\% of apps wrong), less than Jev alone (49.5\%, 1.0\%): the development apps are harder for Jev (38.4\%), and the gain did not transfer, so Jev remains the default.
This is the outcome the development subset exists to catch.
Sonnet~5.5 with the definition is the strongest hosted pricer we tried, 2.6 times Sonnet~5 on all apps (43.2\% against 16.7\%) with no wrong clearance; like Sonnet~5 it is certified only at thresholds up to 0.03, so a looser $\alpha$ does not help it.
After these results we also checked a single sample, which clears at least as much on the development apps (46.4\%): the highest of three samples only raises prices.

\emph{The number of calibration apps.}
By Corollary~\ref{cor:n}, certifying $\alpha=0.05$ at $\varepsilon=0.1$ needs at least 45 programs; Figure~\ref{fig:learning} in the appendix shows that the measured clearance jumps from zero exactly there and grows slowly afterwards.

\answer{\textbf{RQ3:} The unit of the guarantee, the pricer's behavior at the low end of its ranking, and the wording of the question decide the cleared share; the way prices are combined matters less. Cheaper models that answer too confidently clear little, without breaking the guarantee.}

A receipt fact often appears in many receipts, so a few checks by a person or a test can clear many verdicts at once.
We rank facts without looking at any answer (Section~\ref{sec:verify}), let the key answer the top $K$, and certify the threshold again (Table~\ref{tab:verify}).

\answer{\textbf{RQ4:} Receipts make checking efficient: 25 well-chosen facts raise the cleared share from 49\% to 63\% and 100 facts to 78\%, while 100 random facts add three points.}

\emph{Large apps and graceful degradation.}
On equal budgets, 35 minutes and 16\,GB per app, two four-thread runs in parallel on eight cores, the full analysis builds 344 of the 585 apps (59\%), FlowDroid with its models 317 (54\%), and FlowDroid with every unmodeled method opened 252 (43\%): opening the holes costs FlowDroid 65 apps, 46 to the time limit and 19 to memory.
When memory runs out, our pipeline treats bundled libraries as holes (Section~\ref{sec:impl}) and finishes 98 more apps, 442 in all (76\%).
The bundled-library holes, 10{,}163 methods from AndroidX, Kotlin, OkHttp and others, are code that no calibration app contained as holes, yet the threshold certified on the main corpus gives none of the 66 rescued apps a wrong clearance with Jev, and Jev clears 41.5\% of their leak-free verdicts (54.9\% without the 17 apps where R8 moved app code into library classes).
Sonnet's two wrong clearances come from one bundled-library flow it prices at 0.02 (\texttt{AppCompatActivity.setContentView}); on these methods Jev prices absent flows far more confidently than on framework methods (82\% at most 0.05, against 35\%), and both pricers rank them about as well as framework methods (AUC 0.94 and 0.95).
Re-certifying on all 373 apps changes Jev's result little (49.6\% cleared, 1.3\% of held-out apps wrong).
FlowDroid given the same fallback finishes 14 more apps (331) and, on 103 apps with leaks, silently loses 23 of its 308 reported leaks (7.5\%); our fallback instead turns the excluded code into priced holes that the certified bound covers.

\emph{Malware.}
TaintBench documents 203 leaks in 39 malware apps (Table~\ref{tab:taintbench} in the appendix).
With FlowDroid's models, most documented leaks that our analysis reaches are reported directly (83), and only 6 need a flow of an unmodeled method.
All 6 depend on behavior that our flow definition excludes and that the key therefore labels ``no'': three on a callback (\texttt{WebView.}\allowbreak\texttt{addJavascriptInterface}: the app registers an object whose methods return the device identifier, and JavaScript calls them), one on inter-component communication (\texttt{startActivity}) and two on reflection (\texttt{Method.invoke}).
Four of them have cheap receipts and are cleared by every pricer, including counting; the two that pass through reflection have expensive receipts and are reported.
The receipts show that the definition, not the prices, decides these clearances.
Measured against our key, the threshold transfers: no malware app receives a wrong clearance with Jev or Sonnet, against 3 of 23 apps with counting.

\emph{A second population.}
IzzyOnDroid distributes open-source apps that F-Droid does not build itself.
We fixed the list before any run: every app in its index of 15 October 2026 that is not in F-Droid and meets the main corpus's criteria (Section~\ref{sec:setup}), 134 apps.
The same pipeline builds 94 of them, 68 fully and 26 with bundled libraries as holes (the other 40 exceed the budget even then; these apps are larger than F-Droid's), and 83 have a hole-dependent verdict the key decides; the new apps add 2{,}648 unmodeled methods, labeled for the key exactly as in the main corpus.
With the threshold certified on F-Droid, Jev clears 43.7\% of the leak-free verdicts in the fully built apps and 50.6\% in the fallback apps, with no wrong clearance in any of the 83 apps; at $\alpha=0.1$ (threshold 0.3) it clears 60.9\%, again with none.
Sonnet~5.5 with the written definition clears 36.6\%, also without a wrong clearance.

\emph{Other kinds of holes.}
Receipts apply to any gap that can be written as a set of candidate edges.
As a test, we added edges for inter-component communication, from each Intent an app sends to each component that could receive it, with a key from explicit targets and manifest filters.
On 72 apps, 1{,}467 receipts contain such an edge, and the certified threshold clears 40.2\% of the hard slice at $\alpha=0.1$ with 1.4\% of apps wrong.

\answer{\textbf{RQ5:} A threshold certified on F-Droid apps gives no wrong clearance on 66 larger apps whose bundled libraries are holes, nor on 23 malware apps, and receipts extend to inter-component holes. Few documented malware leaks depend on unmodeled library flows; the six that do pass through callbacks, inter-component calls or reflection, which the flow definition excludes, and their receipts make this visible.}
