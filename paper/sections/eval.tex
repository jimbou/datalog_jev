\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

Our evaluation answers six questions:
\begin{description}
\item[RQ1:] How often do clean verdicts depend on library flows that the analysis does not model?
\item[RQ2:] How many of these verdicts do priced receipts clear at a certified risk, compared with the alternatives?
\item[RQ3:] Does the guarantee hold when the answer key changes?
\item[RQ4:] What makes a pricer useful?
\item[RQ5:] Can checking a few receipt facts clear more verdicts?
\item[RQ6:] Does a threshold certified on benign apps hold on malware?
\end{description}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Apps.}
We downloaded about 580 open-source apps from F-Droid and built flow graphs for the 332 that fit our memory and time limits.
The analysis uses FlowDroid's models, and holes are the flows of the methods FlowDroid does not model.
In 307 apps at least one clean sink depends on such holes.
These apps contain 28{,}206 clean, hole-dependent sinks whose status the main key decides: 22{,}888 of them are real leaks that the analysis misses, and 5{,}318, in 298 apps, never receive sensitive data.
We call these 5{,}318 sinks the \emph{hard slice}: they are leak-free, but a sound treatment of unmodeled methods cannot clear them.
For RQ6 we use TaintBench~\cite{taintbench}, 39 malware apps with 203 documented leaks; we built graphs for 33 of them.

\paragraph{Answer keys.}
A wrong clearance is defined relative to a key, so we use several keys built in different ways.
\begin{itemize}
\item The \emph{main key} uses FlowDroid's hand-written models where they exist. For the 12{,}274 methods FlowDroid does not model, it uses the labels on which two language models from different families, Sonnet~5 and GPT-6.1, agree when both are given the written definition of Section~\ref{sec:flow}; where they disagree, it uses K2 (below) if K2 decides the fact, and otherwise leaves the fact unknown.
\item The \emph{deletion keys} contain no language-model labels. They take FlowDroid's models as the truth and delete a share of the modeled classes; the deleted classes become the holes, priced as if unmodeled, and all other modeled classes stay code. Deleting whole classes prevents a pricer from copying the answer of a sibling method.
\item \emph{K2} uses summaries that StubDroid~\cite{stubdroid} generates from the Android~13 and Java~8 source code.
\item The \emph{Sonnet} and \emph{GPT} keys label the unmodeled methods with one model each, asked a short question without the written definition.
\item The \emph{audited key} replaces the labels of the 446 facts that all accepted verdicts rest on by the answers of an audit, in which a person read the Android source code of 67 facts.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Protocol.}
We split the apps at random into two halves 200 times.
In each split, we certify a threshold on one half ($\varepsilon=0.1$, thresholds from 0.01 to 0.5) and measure two numbers on the other half: the share of the hard slice that is cleared, and the share of apps with at least one wrong clearance.
We report the means over the 200 splits.
The guarantee concerns the second number: it should stay below $\alpha$.

\subsection{RQ1: Dependence on Unmodeled Library Flows}
\label{sec:rq1}

To measure how much a taint analysis relies on library models, we ran FlowDroid on 80 F-Droid apps twice: with its models and without them.
Table~\ref{tab:fd-none} shows that without its models FlowDroid loses 82\% of the leaking sinks it reports, and finds no leak that it does not also find with them.
Models are what makes the analysis find leaks, but they are incomplete.
In our corpus, 12{,}274 of the 17{,}830 library methods that lie on a source-to-sink path have no FlowDroid model.
With FlowDroid's models, our analysis reports 49{,}007 sinks as leaks, while 22{,}888 further real leaks, a third of all, are reachable only through unmodeled methods and are reported as clean.
Treating every unmodeled method as passing all data finds them, but adds 5{,}318 false alarms in 298 of the 307 apps.
On TaintBench, the documented leaks are mostly reachable through modeled code: only 6 of the 89 documented leaks our analysis reaches need an unmodeled flow, and all 6 go through callbacks, inter-component communication or reflection (RQ6).

The dependence is not specific to our analysis.
We gave FlowDroid holes as well: FlowDroid reads library behavior from summary files, so opening every hole means adding a summary with every candidate flow for each method it does not model, next to its own summaries.
On the 120 apps where all of FlowDroid's runs finished within 20 minutes, open holes add 58 reported sinks in 19 apps.
For 43 of them, switching off the hole methods on the reported paths and running FlowDroid again removes the sink, so FlowDroid itself confirms the receipt.

\answer{\textbf{RQ1:} Library models matter (FlowDroid loses 82\% of its leaks without them) and are incomplete: even with FlowDroid's models, a third of the real leaks in our corpus pass only through unmodeled methods. Opening these methods finds the leaks but adds false alarms to almost every app.}

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the cleared share for each pricer, and Figure~\ref{fig:curve} the whole trade-off between clearing and wrong clearances.
With Jev's prices, receipts clear 49\% of the hard slice at $\alpha=0.05$, and 0.9\% of held-out apps receive a wrong clearance; at $\alpha=0.02$ they still clear 37\%.
The analysis then reports the real leaks through unmodeled methods whose receipts are expensive, while half of the false alarms that opening these methods would add are cleared.
In every row the observed error stays below $\alpha$, as Proposition~\ref{prop:ltt} predicts; what differs is how much is cleared.

\emph{Counting assumptions clears nothing.}
Counting gives every receipt of size one the same bound, so the first threshold already clears every one-fact receipt, and that puts a wrong clearance in 87\% of the apps.
The calibration therefore refuses every threshold.
Prices are what make receipts usable.

\emph{A language model's answer taken as fact has no risk control.}
Taking the model's yes/no answers as library models clears 80 to 90\% of the hard slice on the deletion keys, but 3.5\% of the apps receive a wrong clearance on average and up to 7.7\% in some runs, above a 5\% target in 9 of 18 runs.
There is one operating point, and its error cannot be chosen.

\emph{The unit of the guarantee matters.}
Table~\ref{tab:unit} compares our per-program calibration with conformal selection over verdicts~\cite{conformalselection}, which controls the share of wrong verdicts among the cleared ones.
At a 5\% target, conformal selection clears more, 74.8\%, but it misses its own target, with 6.6\% of the cleared verdicts wrong (18.0\% at a 10\% target), because verdicts in one app share facts and the apps are not alike.
Its wrong verdicts are spread over 53 of the 307 apps, so a user reading one app's report would see a wrong clearance in one app out of six.
Using the bound directly as a probability, without calibration, is safe for Jev but clears only 11.8\%, because its prices are conservative.
The certified result changes little with the confidence parameter, the size of the calibration half, or when apps that bundle the same libraries are kept on the same side of each split.
In a few splits the held-out share of wrong apps exceeds 5\%, as the bracket in Table~\ref{tab:unit} shows; the guarantee bounds how often this happens (at most a share $\varepsilon$ of calibrations), not each split.

\subsection{RQ3: Robustness to the Answer Key}
\label{sec:rq3}

Every answer key can be wrong, so we repeat the calibration under each key in Table~\ref{tab:keys}.
The guarantee holds under all of them: the share of held-out apps with a wrong clearance stays at or below 2.3\% at $\alpha=0.05$.
The cleared share, however, depends on the key, from about 25\% under the keys with single-model labels to 49\% under the main key and 93\% under K2, which decides fewer facts and calls most of them ``no''.
Flipping 11\% of the Sonnet key's ``no'' labels at random, the error rate a person's check of these labels suggested, lowers the cleared share to a few percent without breaking the guarantee.

\emph{Is the key independent of the pricer?}
Sonnet~5 is both a pricer and one of the two labelers of the main key.
Under the key built from Sonnet's own labels alone, Sonnet clears less than under the main key (9.1\% against 16.7\%), and under the keys with no Sonnet labels, the deletion keys and K2, it clears as much as Jev or more, so agreement with itself does not explain its results.

\emph{Does the key follow the definition?}
The disagreement between Sonnet and GPT comes from the question, not from the models.
Asked the short question, the two models agree on 81\% of the candidate flows (Cohen's $\kappa=0.62$), and they agree with a person's source-code checks on 81\% and 90\% of the facts.
Asked again with the written definition and one reason code per answer (Appendix~\ref{app:flow}), they agree on 90\% of 37{,}160 candidate flows ($\kappa=0.80$), both call about 41\% of the flows real, and they agree with the person on 92\% and 93\% of the 60 facts; on the 57 facts where both models give the same answer, the person agrees with that answer 54 times (95\%).
This is why the main key uses the answers on which both models agree under the written definition.

\emph{The deletion keys test pricers on code they have not seen.}
Figure~\ref{fig:deletion} shows the cleared share as more classes are deleted.
A classifier trained on FlowDroid's models is strong when single methods are deleted, because it learns the conventions of the remaining sibling methods, and collapses when whole classes are deleted, which is the realistic case: 86\% of the unmodeled methods that appear in receipts belong to classes FlowDroid does not model at all.
Language-model pricers do not depend on siblings and keep their clearance.

\end{figure}

\answer{\textbf{RQ3:} The guarantee holds under all eight keys, including keys with no language-model labels and keys with random label noise. The cleared share depends on how many flows the key calls real; with the written definition, two model families agree with each other ($\kappa=0.80$), and where they agree, with a person's source checks (95\%).}

\label{sec:rq4}

Table~\ref{tab:panel} adds five cheaper language models, asked the same question as Sonnet.
None of them clears much, and the reason is visible in their prices: they answer almost only with extreme values (Qwen3.8-27B gives 0.02 or 0.95 to 97\% of the flows) and give the lowest price to some real flows.
The lowest threshold that clears anything then already puts a wrong clearance in many apps, and the calibration refuses it.
The guarantee still holds: a poor pricer costs clearance, not validity.

\emph{Average ranking quality is not what counts.}
Table~\ref{tab:rank} shows that the classifier and Sonnet rank verdicts almost as well as Jev on average (AUROC 0.965 and 0.950 against 0.970), yet clear far less at $\alpha=0.05$ (24\% and 17\% against 49\%).
By Proposition~\ref{prop:order}, only the order of the bounds matters, and only at the low end, where verdicts are cleared: one real flow with a low price is enough to make an app wrong.
A pricer for receipts should be judged by its curve near the origin of Figure~\ref{fig:curve}, not by an average score.

\emph{Stating the flow rule in the question matters.}
Jev's clearance at $\alpha=0.05$ is 8.5\% when asked whether taint propagates, and 48.7\% when the question states that a tainted object taints everything read from it; asked only whether a flow exists, it is certified in 2 of 200 splits.
Without the rule, getters on tainted objects receive low prices, and the few real flows among them block the threshold.

\emph{How prices are combined matters less.}
With the same receipts, summing over distinct facts instead of edges clears 51.7\%, the largest single price 33.7\%, and the product under independence 52.3\%, all with at most 1.8\% of apps wrong.
The calibration makes each of these valid; the union bound is the one that is also a guaranteed upper bound for each verdict.

\answer{\textbf{RQ4:} A useful pricer gives low prices only to flows that are really absent. Jev clears 49 to 60\% of the hard slice and Sonnet~5 17\%; cheaper models answer too confidently and clear little, without breaking the guarantee. Stating the flow rule in the question raises Jev's clearance from 9\% to 49\%.}

\label{sec:rq5}

\bottomrule

A receipt fact often appears in many receipts, so a few checks by a person, or a test, can clear many verdicts at once.
We rank facts without looking at any answer (Section~\ref{sec:verify}), let the key answer the top $K$, and certify the threshold again.
Table~\ref{tab:verify} shows the result.
Checking the 25 facts that would clear the most verdicts with a ``no'' (the unlock rule) raises the cleared share from 49\% to 63\%, and 100 checks raise it to 78\%.
Ranking by the number of uncleared verdicts a fact blocks clears less (73\%) but turns more facts into confirmed flows, so the analysis reports 13{,}500 more real leaks.
Checking 100 facts chosen at random adds three points, so the ranking is what makes the checks useful.
The held-out error stays below the target in every row.

\answer{\textbf{RQ5:} Receipts make checking efficient: 25 well-chosen facts raise the cleared share from 49\% to 63\% and 100 facts to 78\%, while 100 random facts add three points.}

\subsection{RQ6: Transfer to Malware}
\label{sec:rq6}

Our calibration apps are benign F-Droid apps, while a tool may be used on very different apps.
We therefore certify a threshold on all 307 F-Droid apps and apply it to the TaintBench malware apps.
For each documented leak, we look up our verdicts for the method that contains its sink (Table~\ref{tab:taintbench}).

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{TaintBench: documented leaks against our verdicts (analysis with FlowDroid's models), and the threshold certified on the 307 F-Droid apps applied to the 23 malware apps with hole-dependent verdicts.}
\label{tab:taintbench}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
Documented leaks & 203\\
\quad outside our scope (no graph or no hole-dependent verdict: 28; sink not in the list: 4) & 32\\
\quad reported with FlowDroid's models & 83\\
\quad reachable only through unmodeled flows & 6\\
\quad not reached & 82\\
\midrule
Of the 6: callbacks / inter-component calls / reflection & 3 / 1 / 2\\
Of the 6: cleared by Jev ($\delta=0.2$), Sonnet~5 ($\delta=0.02$) and counting & 4\\
Apps with a wrong clearance (main key), Jev / Sonnet~5 / counting & 0 / 0 / 3 of 23\\
Leak-free hole-dependent verdicts cleared, Jev / Sonnet~5 & 31.8\% / 22.9\%\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

With FlowDroid's models, most documented leaks that our analysis reaches are reported directly (83), and only 6 need a flow of an unmodeled method.
All 6 depend on behavior that our flow definition excludes and that the key therefore labels ``no'': three on a callback (the method \texttt{addJavascriptInterface} of \texttt{WebView}: the app registers an object whose methods return the device identifier, and JavaScript calls them), one on inter-component communication (\texttt{startActivity}) and two on reflection (\texttt{Method.invoke}).
Four of them have cheap receipts and are cleared by every pricer, including counting; the two that pass through reflection have expensive receipts and are reported.
The receipts show exactly which assumption each clearance shares, so a user who wants such leaks reported can see that the definition, not the prices, decides them.
Measured against our key, the threshold transfers: no malware app receives a wrong clearance with Jev or Sonnet, against 3 of 23 apps with counting, and Jev clears 32\% of the leak-free hole-dependent verdicts.

\answer{\textbf{RQ6:} Measured against our key, a threshold certified on benign apps held on 23 malware apps. Few documented malware leaks depend on unmodeled library flows; the six that do pass through callbacks, inter-component calls or reflection, which our definition of a flow excludes, and their receipts make this visible.}
