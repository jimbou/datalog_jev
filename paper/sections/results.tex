\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
We ask: is unsoundness pervasive (RQ1); does priced-receipt clearing beat a sound over-approximation, and does the guarantee hold (RQ2); how do pricers compare, and does the key matter (RQ3); how good is the key behind the clearances (RQ4); how faithful is our analysis to FlowDroid (RQ5); what does it cost (RQ6); do the validity checks hold up (RQ7); which design choices matter (RQ8); which assumptions a person should check (RQ9); and whether it matters for real leaks in malware (RQ10).

\subsection{RQ1: Is unsoundness pervasive?}
\label{sec:rq1}
Of the 17{,}830 framework methods on source-to-sink paths, 12{,}274 are not modeled by FlowDroid.
We ran FlowDroid on 80 random small apps with and without its library summaries (\texttt{-tw NONE}).
Over the 69 apps with both answers, 127 of the 154 leaking sink methods FlowDroid reports with its summaries (82\%) disappear without them, and none appears only without them (Table~\ref{tab:fd-none}).
This is the extreme case: it measures how much a library model matters, not what FlowDroid misses with its default summaries.

\subsection{RQ2: Does it clear more than a sound analysis, and does the guarantee hold?}

The three wrong clearances at the chosen threshold come from three different facts and apps, not from one widely bundled fact (Figure~\ref{fig:receipt}).

The cheaper models answer almost only with extreme values (Qwen3.8-27B gives 0.02 or 0.95 to 97\% of the candidate flows) and put some real flows at the lowest price, so the lowest certifiable threshold already makes too many apps wrong and calibration refuses, or certifies only in a few splits; Proposition~\ref{prop:ltt} still bounds the probability of such a split by $\varepsilon$.
An overconfident pricer therefore costs clearance, not validity.

\subsection{RQ4: How good is the answer key behind the clearances?}
\label{sec:rq4}
All verdicts accepted at the chosen threshold rest on 446 distinct library facts (Table~\ref{tab:facts}).
A first, loosely worded AI pass disagreed with the key on 85 facts; a written, stricter rule (ordinary documented behavior, no custom overrides, influence is not a flow) resolved 86 of its 95 positives, and agreement with the key rose from 78\% to 97.7\%.
Because that rule was written after seeing the first pass, we report both.
A person read the Android source for the 9 positives and the 18 undecided facts (all 9 confirmed; 11 of 18 became negative) and for 40 facts the AI called negative (12 chosen for impact, 28 at random): 4 were flows, all of the kind where the method keeps a reference to its argument; the estimated miss rate among the AI's negatives is about 11\% (95\% interval 2--28\%).
Calibrating under the key with all of these answers is identical to the main key to the last digit (39.4\% cleared, 2.0\% wrong), because every path through those facts also needs another candidate flow the key keeps absent.
K2 decides 176 of the 446 facts (45\% of their use), all as no flow, and agrees with the key on 173; it is not a perfect witness (it misses the prefix flow in \texttt{File.createTempFile}).

\subsection{RQ5: How faithful is our analysis to FlowDroid?}
\label{sec:rq5}
On the 70 of 80 apps where FlowDroid gave an answer (30 found no leak, 18 stopped at its own memory limit), it reports 175 leaking sink methods; our analysis reaches 102 (58\%) with every hole on and 80 (46\%) under the key.
The misses are mostly inter-component sinks (\texttt{startActivityForResult}, \texttt{bindService}), file and network calls, and code in bundled libraries.
In the other direction we are far less precise: we flag 8{,}184 key-true sink methods, and FlowDroid also reports 80 (1\%).
On the first 40 apps, restricting the analysis to code reachable from entry points removed only 11.5\% of these and did not change the recall, so dead code is not the cause; the whole-program, context-insensitive design is.
Our analysis is therefore a weaker tool than FlowDroid; the guarantee is about flows missed through library holes relative to it, which is why RQ10 measures it on documented leaks.

\subsection{RQ7: Do the validity checks hold up?}
\label{sec:rq7}
\paragraph{The unit of the guarantee.}
Table~\ref{tab:unit} compares calibration units on the same bounds.
Conformal selection over verdicts with leave-one-app-out p-values and Benjamini--Hochberg~\cite{conformalselection} meets its target on this corpus (2.6\% of selected verdicts wrong at a 5\% target; on our first, smaller corpus it did not: 7.9\%) and clears more (55.6\%).
But its wrong verdicts are spread out: 166 of the 324 apps (51\%) contain at least one wrong clearance, and 62 (19\%) even at a 1\% target.
Per-program calibration leaves a wrong clearance in 1.0\% of held-out apps.
Using the bound at face value ($\delta=\alpha$, no calibration) is safe here but clears only 5.5\%: Jev's prices are conservative, and calibration recovers what they leave on the table.

The grouped splits link apps that share at least three third-party package prefixes and keep each cluster on one side (196 clusters, the largest with 57 apps); the result does not change, so shared libraries do not inflate it.
\paragraph{Min-cut optimality.}
Solving the exact ``any correlation'' problem as a linear program over all $2^m$ worlds on 190 random graphs with 5 to 11 hole edges gives the max-flow value to within $2\cdot10^{-15}$, while the probability under independence is lower by 0.08 to 0.17 on average, so an independence-based bound understates the risk.
\paragraph{How many calibration apps are needed.}
With no wrong app among $n$ calibration apps, the exact test certifies $\alpha$ only if $(1-\alpha)^n\le\varepsilon$, so $n\ge\ln\varepsilon/\ln(1-\alpha)$: 22 apps for $\alpha=0.1$, 45 for $0.05$ and 114 for $0.02$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:n}).
Figure~\ref{fig:learning} shows that the certified clearance jumps from nothing to its full value exactly at these sizes and is flat afterwards: beyond the floor, more calibration apps do not buy more clearance, they only tighten the spread.

\subsection{RQ8: Which design choices matter?}
\label{sec:rq8}
\paragraph{How prices combine.}
With the same cuts and the same calibration, the sum over cut edges clears 40.0\%, the sum over distinct facts 43.0\%, the largest single price 46.8\%, and the independence product 45.3\%, all with at most 1.9\% of held-out apps wrong.
Validity comes from the calibration, not from the union bound; the union bound buys an interpretation, and counting each fact once is valid and free.
\paragraph{Question wording.}
Stating the object convention (a tainted object taints everything read from it) in the question nearly triples the certified clearance on the full corpus, from 14.2\% to 40.0\% at $\alpha=0.05$ (Table~\ref{tab:wording}); every wording stays within the target.

\subsection{RQ9: Which assumptions should a person check?}
\label{sec:rq9}
Receipts name the facts that block clearance, so a person (or a test) can check a few of them.
We rank facts without looking at any answer, let the key answer the top $K$, and recompute and recertify (Section~\ref{sec:verify}).

Twenty-five checked facts lift the certified clearance from 40\% to 53\% and more than double the real leaks the analysis reports; one hundred random facts change nothing, so the ranking is what matters.
Checks work both ways: a ``no'' removes a hole and can clear sinks outright, a ``yes'' turns a library flow into code, so real leaks become reported and leave the hard slice.
Our first rule, which assumes the answer is ``no'', is beaten at 100 facts by simply counting the uncleared verdicts whose receipt contains the fact (64.2\% against 58.0\%).
Under K2, which answers ``no'' to most facts, 50 checks lift clearance from 25.5\% to 78.7\%.
Held-out error stays within the target throughout.

\subsection{RQ10: Does it matter for real leaks?}
\label{sec:rq10}
TaintBench~\cite{taintbench} documents 203 leaks in 39 Android malware apps.
We built graphs for 33 of them with TaintBench's source and sink list, priced the 108 framework methods they add with the same pricers, and matched each documented leak to our verdicts at the method that contains its sink (Table~\ref{tab:taintbench}).
\begin{table}[t]
\caption{TaintBench: documented leaks against our verdicts, and the threshold certified on the 324 F-Droid apps applied to the 31 malware apps with hole-dependent verdicts.}
\label{tab:taintbench}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
Documented leaks & 203\\
\quad out of scope (no graph: 24; sink not in the list: 4) & 28\\
\quad reported without any library hole & 44\\
\quad reachable only through library holes & 48\\
\quad not reached & 83\\
\midrule
Cleared at the F-Droid threshold, Jev ($\delta=0.3$) / Sonnet~5 ($\delta=0.03$) & 3 / 3\\
Apps with a wrong clearance (key), Jev / Sonnet~5 & 0 / 1 of 31\\
Leak-free hole-dependent verdicts cleared, Jev / Sonnet~5 & 35.6\% / 30.2\%\\
Counting, receipts of one fact: leaks cleared; apps wrong & 22; 21 of 31\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}
Of the 92 documented leaks our analysis reaches, 48 (52\%) need a library flow it does not model: on real malware, half of what the unsound analysis can see is assumed away.
The threshold certified on F-Droid apps transfers: no malware app has a wrong clearance by our key with Jev, and one of 31 with Sonnet~5.
Three documented leaks are cleared by both pricers, all in one app and all resting on one receipt fact, \texttt{WebView.addJavascriptInterface} (receiver $\to$ argument): the app injects an object whose methods return the device identifier, and the leak happens when JavaScript calls them, a callback our flow definition excludes and our key labels ``no''.
The receipt is what makes this miss explainable: one checkable fact, shared by all three.
Clearing every receipt of one fact without prices would clear 22 documented leaks.
