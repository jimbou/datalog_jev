Table~\ref{tab:related} places our approach among prior answers to unmodeled library code.
Prior work either trusts its source of library facts or gives it no role; our approach uses an untrusted source and certifies the result.

\paragraph{Soundiness.}
The soundiness manifesto~\cite{soundiness} asks analysis designers to state the features they do not handle soundly.
We take the next step for one such feature, library code: each clean verdict states which library facts it assumes, and the analysis states how often such verdicts are wrong, with a certified bound.
Definition~\ref{def:soundy} turns ``soundy'' from a list of omissions into a measurable property.

\paragraph{Library models.}
FlowDroid's hand-written summaries~\cite{flowdroid} cover about 350 classes.
StubDroid~\cite{stubdroid} generates summaries from library code, DAInfer~\cite{dainfer} infers aliasing specifications from documentation, Atlas~\cite{atlas} infers specifications from library executions, Bastani et al.~\cite{bastani-popl15} infer points-to specifications, and Merlin~\cite{merlin} and Seldon~\cite{seldon} infer which methods are sources, sinks or sanitizers.
More models make an analysis less unsound but never sound, because some methods remain unmodeled or wrongly modeled, and none of these approaches says how often its verdicts are wrong.
Receipts are complementary: they start from the models, here FlowDroid's, and price exactly the facts that the models leave open, so better models leave fewer holes to price.
We use StubDroid as an independent answer key (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}).

\paragraph{Language models as library oracles.}
IRIS~\cite{iris} and SemTaint~\cite{semtaint} ask language models for taint specifications of library methods and use the answers as facts; LLMDFA~\cite{llmdfa} has a language model extract sources, sinks and dataflow summaries, and CPGHunter~\cite{cpghunter} pairs language-model source and sink specifications with semantic models of library APIs; all trust the answers.
This is the ``model taken as fact'' baseline in our evaluation: it clears more than our approach but its wrong clearances cannot be controlled (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}).
We use language models in the same role but only as pricers: their answers rank verdicts, and the calibration, not the model, provides the guarantee, so a poor model clears less instead of producing wrong clearances (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).
NeuroLog~\cite{neurolog} feeds model-extracted facts to Datalog to find bugs; we use the same kind of combination to bound missed flows.

\paragraph{Minimal assumptions behind a verdict.}
Bastani et al.~\cite{bastani-oopsla15} compute minimal cuts in an information-flow graph, where each cut is a set of statements assumed unreachable, and ask an analyst to confirm them by testing.
Zhu et al.~\cite{zhu-aplas13} infer the library specifications that a source-sink property needs, and abductive inference~\cite{dillig-pldi12,elrond} finds the facts that would complete a proof.
These approaches tell the user what to check but not how likely the assumptions are to fail, so every verdict needs a person.
Our receipts are such cuts with prices, and the certified threshold decides which verdicts need no person at all; counting the assumptions without prices clears nothing at any useful risk (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}).
When a person is available, receipts also tell which facts to check first (Section~\ref{sec:rq4}).

\paragraph{Probabilistic program analysis.}
Praline~\cite{praline} computes sound bounds on the probabilities of Datalog outputs when input facts have known probabilities and partly known correlations.
For reachability with only individual probabilities known, its optimum is the min-cut (Proposition~\ref{prop:tight}), which we compute directly with max-flow.
Probabilistic inference gives a bound per verdict but no guarantee that the input probabilities are right; our calibration adds this guarantee and remains valid when the probabilities are wrong.

\paragraph{Distribution-free risk control.}
Our threshold uses fixed-sequence testing from Learn-then-Test~\cite{lt}.
Conformal selection~\cite{conformalselection} controls the share of wrong selections among selected items and is the natural verdict-level alternative.
For program analysis the unit matters: at the same 5\% target, verdict-level selection left wrong clearances in 17\% of the apps, while per-program calibration left them in 0.9\% (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).
To our knowledge, this is the first application of distribution-free risk control to the verdicts of a static analysis.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Unsound static analyses report clean verdicts that silently depend on library code.
We showed how to make this dependence explicit and measurable: each clean verdict carries a receipt of the library facts it assumes, a pricer bounds the chance that any of them is real, and a threshold certified on whole programs makes the analysis $(\alpha,\varepsilon)$-soundy for any pricer.
On 307 Android apps, receipts on top of FlowDroid's models clear half of the false alarms that opening unmodeled methods adds, at a 5\% risk per program, and checking a few receipt facts clears most of the rest.
The limits are equally clear: the guarantee is relative to an answer key and to the analysis it is applied to, and it needs a pricer that gives low prices only to flows that are really absent.
